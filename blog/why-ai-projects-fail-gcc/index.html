Why AI Projects Fail in the GCC — and What Works

15 October 2026 · 5 min read · Khatib Designs

Why AI projects fail in the GCC is rarely a story about the model. In almost every stalled pilot we are asked to review in Kuwait and the Gulf, the cause was decided in the first two weeks — scope, data access, measurement or language — and discovered six months later. Here are the four patterns that account for most of it, and what a project that survives looks like instead.

Failure one: the pilot never touched a real system

The most expensive failure is the assistant that works beautifully on a spreadsheet export and was never connected to anything. It answers confidently about last quarter's price list, because that is the file somebody emailed over at kickoff. To answer a customer today it needs read access to the ERP, the live stock table or the booking system — and that access was either out of scope or still sitting with IT when the budget ran out. Most production assistants are retrieval-based: they answer from your own records rather than from memory, so the integration is not a later phase of the project, it is the product. Decide in week one which system it reads from, who signs the access off, and what it tells a customer when that system is unreachable. On WCS, a factory timesheet, wages and budget system, the value only appeared once the software matched how the floor actually recorded hours rather than how the process was documented. The same gap is what strands AI pilots.

Failure two: no baseline, so no verdict

A pilot with no baseline cannot be defended at budget time. It feels faster loses to a spreadsheet every November. Before anything is built, pick one number and measure it manually for a week: average minutes to answer an enquiry, share of tickets resolved without escalation, invoices processed per person per day, days sales outstanding. That single figure decides renewal far more than the demo did. Two expectations usually need resetting at the same time — what the work costs and how long it takes. We publish both honestly: AI automation pricing in Kuwait and a realistic AI project timeline. A six-week budget attached to a six-month ambition kills more Gulf projects than bad engineering does.

Failure three: the wrong tool, in both directions

A large share of failed GCC initiatives are a company-wide chat subscription and nothing else. It is genuinely useful for drafting an email in two languages and structurally unable to tell a customer where their order is, because it has never seen your order table. The opposite error costs just as much: commissioning a custom build for something a licensed product already does well, then maintaining it forever. The honest comparison is in ChatGPT vs a custom AI assistant, and the test takes a minute. Does a correct answer depend on your own private data, and does a wrong answer cost you money or trust? Two yeses means you need something built, grounded and governed. Two noes means buy licences, train the team, and spend the project money somewhere it matters.

Failure four: tested in English, sold to Arabic-speaking customers

This pattern is specific to this market and it is brutally common. The pilot is evaluated by an English-speaking project team, scores well in the review meeting, then meets customers who write in Gulf dialect, drop English product names into Arabic sentences, or type Arabic in Latin letters. Accuracy falls, staff stop trusting it, and the rollout quietly stops. Build the test set from real messages pulled out of your own WhatsApp, Instagram and helpdesk history — three hundred is enough — and label the correct answer for each before launch. A bulk-translated knowledge base is a separate trap: the Arabic policy reads fluently and means something slightly different from the English one, which is why Arabic AI translation needs a review step by someone who knows the business, not a one-off machine pass. If your customers are Arabic-first, the assistant has to be too.

What a project that survives looks like

The pattern is consistent and unglamorous. One narrow use case with high volume and repetitive questions. One owner inside the business with the authority to approve answers. Access to the real system agreed in writing in week one. A measured baseline. A weekly half hour spent reading transcripts for the first two months, turning the three most common failures into answers. A running-cost line in the budget, because model usage, channel fees and content maintenance continue after handover. And an explicit kill criterion, so a pilot that is not working can be stopped without it becoming a political defeat. McKinsey research on the state of AI points the same way: value concentrates where companies redesign the workflow around the tool, not where they bolt the tool onto the workflow and hope. If a pilot has stalled and you want a second opinion before spending more on it, that is exactly the kind of scoping our AI consultancy and build work starts with.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common reason AI projects fail in the GCC?

Lack of access to real company data. The pilot is built on an exported file, proves nothing about live operations, and stalls when integration turns out to be the real work. Agree in week one which system the assistant reads from and who approves that access.

How do we know whether our AI pilot actually worked?

Pick one operational number before you build — minutes per enquiry, share of conversations resolved without a human, invoices per person per day — and measure it manually for a week as a baseline. Without that baseline there is no verdict, only opinion.

Is a custom AI assistant always better than an off-the-shelf tool?

No. If a correct answer depends on your own private data and a wrong answer costs money or trust, you need something built and governed. If neither is true, buy licences and spend the project budget elsewhere.
