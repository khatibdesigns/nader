AI Pilot Project: Test AI in 6 Weeks Before You Build

19 November 2026 · 4 min read · Nader Alkhatib

An AI pilot project is the cheapest way to find out whether AI will actually work inside your business — before you commit to a full build. In Kuwait and the wider GCC, most of the wasted AI budget we see was not spent on bad technology; it was spent on good technology pointed at the wrong workflow. A pilot catches that mistake for a fraction of the price.

What an AI pilot project actually is

An AI pilot project is a short, scoped build — usually four to six weeks — that puts a working AI system in front of real users, running on your real data, and measures one number against a baseline recorded before the build started. That last part is what separates a pilot from a demo. A demo proves a model can produce plausible output. A pilot proves the output survives contact with your staff, your customers, your Arabic and your edge cases.

It is also not a proof of concept that lives on an engineer's laptop. Three things make something a pilot rather than a science project: real data (not a cleaned sample), real users (the same people who would use it in production), and a pass mark agreed in advance. If any of the three is missing, you will finish the engagement holding an opinion instead of a decision. McKinsey's annual survey of the state of AI has repeatedly found that the companies reporting real bottom-line impact are the ones that redesigned the surrounding workflow — not the ones that bought the best tool. A pilot is where you find out what the workflow needs to become. It is the first stage of most of the AI work we take on, and we would rather sell you one than a twelve-month roadmap built on guesses.

Pick one workflow where a number can move

The scope of a pilot should be narrow enough that a single metric either moves or it doesn't. Useful candidates in GCC businesses:

  • Quotations — hours from enquiry received to priced quote sent.
  • Customer messages — share of WhatsApp or Instagram enquiries resolved without a human, in Arabic and English.
  • Documents — minutes per invoice, LPO or delivery note entered into your system, plus the error rate.
  • Claims and calculations — for a shipping agent, the hours needed to produce a defensible laytime and demurrage calculation from the statement of facts.
  • Internal answers — time staff spend hunting through policies, specs or price lists before they can reply.

Avoid pilots phrased as use AI in marketing or make the company more efficient. Those cannot fail, which means they cannot succeed either. Narrow is not timid; narrow is what makes the result believable to a finance director.

Record the baseline before anyone writes code

Pull 50 to 100 real cases from the last quarter — actual customer messages, actual invoices, actual quote requests, including the ugly ones. Measure how long they take today, how often they go wrong, and what each one costs in staff time. Half a day of this work is the most valuable half day in the whole project, because it is the only thing that makes week six conclusive.

Then write the pass mark down in one sentence. For example: 70% of enquiries answered correctly with no human escalation, measured on 100 real Arabic and English conversations. Write the hard limits too — wrong prices, invented policies, or Arabic that reads like machine translation are automatic failures regardless of the headline number. In Gulf dialects especially, a system that is accurate but sounds foreign will be rejected by customers before anyone looks at your dashboard.

What a pilot costs, and how to contract it

Price it as a fixed fee for a fixed scope with named deliverables and a dated end. In Kuwait, a properly scoped pilot on one workflow typically lands between KD 1,500 and KD 5,000, including integration with one existing system, plus a modest monthly running cost for model usage and hosting. Much cheaper is usually a prompt in a browser tab; much more expensive is a production build wearing a pilot's name.

Get four things in writing before you sign: who owns the code, prompts and evaluation data; where your data is processed and how long it is retained; what you receive if the answer is no; and who does the hardening if the answer is yes. The go/no-go decision at week six

A pilot has three honest endings. Scale — the number moved past the pass mark and the workflow is worth rebuilding around. Re-scope — the technology worked but the chosen workflow was the wrong one, and you now know which neighbouring process to try. Stop — the data is too thin, the process too inconsistent, or the saving too small to bother. Stopping after a KD 3,000 pilot is a good outcome; discovering the same thing after a KD 30,000 build is not.

If you do proceed, budget for the unglamorous half: monitoring, logging, a clean fallback to a human, access control, and integration with your systems of record. That work is usually where the real timeline sits. Our build for factory timesheets, wages and budgets followed the same path — one workflow, measured against what it replaced, then widened only once the numbers held.

Frequently asked questions

How long should an AI pilot project take?

Four to six weeks is the right window for one workflow. Shorter than that and you cannot gather enough real cases to judge accuracy; longer than that and you are funding a production build without having earned the decision to start one.

What should an AI pilot project cost in Kuwait?

A fixed-scope pilot on a single workflow usually costs between KD 1,500 and KD 5,000, including integration with one existing system, plus a small monthly cost for model usage and hosting. The fee should be fixed and the end date dated before work begins.

What happens if the pilot fails?

You keep the baseline data, the evaluation set and a clear written reason it failed — which is worth more than a vague success. Agree up front that you own that material, and that a no ends the engagement without further commitment.
