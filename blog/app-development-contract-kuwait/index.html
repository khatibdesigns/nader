App Development Contract Kuwait: What to Check

9 November 2026 · 5 min read · Khatib Designs

An app development contract Kuwait buyers sign is usually the only document that survives a disagreement — and most disputes we are asked to clean up are definitional, not technical: both sides agreed on a price and never agreed on what the price bought.

What the contract must define before you sign

An app development contract Kuwait buyers sign is the document that decides who pays when something is late, missing or wrong. Before you sign, make sure five things are written in plain language. If any one of them is missing, the number in the proposal is an estimate dressed up as a commitment.

  • The deliverable — named screens, named features, named platforms. Not a booking app.
  • The acceptance test — how both sides decide the work is done.
  • Ownership — of source code, designs, accounts and data.
  • The payment trigger — what event releases each instalment.
  • After launch — who fixes what, how fast, at what monthly cost.

Fees matter less than these five. A cheap contract with no acceptance clause costs more than an expensive one with a tight scope, because the cheap one is paid for twice. Scope and acceptance: the two clauses that prevent arguments

Attach a scope document — formally a statement of work — as an annex, and reference it by date and version in the contract body. It should list every screen, every user role, every integration and every automated job. Two details that get skipped in Kuwait specifically: an Arabic and English app is two layouts, not one translated one, because right-to-left changes navigation, forms and charts; and any KNET or payment step depends on a bank timeline you do not control, so payment gateway integration belongs in its own milestone with its own dates.

Acceptance should be a test, not an opinion. Name the devices, the test accounts, the data set and the pass condition — for example, a user completes a booking and receives both notifications on a mid-range Android handset on a Kuwaiti SIM. Then set a review window: seven working days to raise defects, after which the milestone is accepted. On an enterprise build like our factory timesheet and wages system, acceptance was written around payroll rules rather than screens, because a wrong wage calculation is the only failure that actually matters there.

Change requests need a clause too, and it should be boring: changes are quoted in writing, priced, and either approved or dropped. Teams that handle changes informally are the teams whose projects slip by months.

Who owns the code, the data and the AI

Ask for full assignment of intellectual property on final payment of each milestone, delivery of the Git repository, and a written handover list. The non-negotiable part in 2026 is accounts: cloud, app store, analytics, domain, database and model API keys should live in entities your company owns, with the agency added as a user it can remove. Agencies that hold your App Store account hold your product.

If the build includes AI, the contract needs three extra lines. Which model or vendor is used, and who pays for tokens. Where customer data is processed and whether it can be used for training — state no unless you mean yes. And what happens if the vendor changes its terms or prices, which is a question of substitution rights, not of blame. Payment milestones and the support ladder

Pay against delivered, testable milestones — never against elapsed time. A workable pattern for a Kuwait build is 25% to mobilise, 25% on an approved design and scope annex, 30% on a working build in staging that passes the acceptance test, and 20% on release plus handover of code and accounts. Retention of the final 20% is what keeps attention on your project in its last, most fiddly month.

Then state the support arrangement inline, as a ladder with monthly numbers, not as a vague promise of help. Ours runs in three steps, and it is the only recurring figure in our terms — we quote fixed terms rather than open-ended retainers for build work.

  • Care — KWD 150–300 per month. Crash and bug fixes, OS and library updates, store compliance, uptime monitoring. No new features.
  • Care plus changes — KWD 400–750 per month. Everything above, plus a change queue and roughly one release a month.
  • Partner — KWD 900–1,800 per month. Everything above, plus managed AI and infrastructure spend, agreed response times and reserved engineering hours.

Attach response times to the tier you pick — a one-line service-level agreement is enough: critical issues acknowledged within four working hours, resolved or worked around within two working days. Five red flags

  • A fixed price with no scope annex attached.
  • Acceptance described as client satisfaction, with no test.
  • IP transferring only after an undefined project completion.
  • Accounts and keys held in the agency's name.
  • No exit clause — you should be able to end the engagement, pay for accepted work, and leave with the code.

None of this requires a lawyer to draft from scratch. It requires an annex, five defined clauses and a support tier chosen on purpose before the first invoice, rather than negotiated after the first problem.

Frequently asked questions

Does an app development contract in Kuwait need to be in Arabic?

A bilingual contract is common and useful: Kuwaiti courts work in Arabic, so if the agreement may ever be enforced locally, prepare an Arabic version and state in the contract which language prevails. The technical scope annex is usually kept in English because the terminology is.

How much of the fee should be held until handover?

Keep 20% of the total tied to release plus delivery of source code, accounts and documentation. That retention is the single most effective clause for making sure the last ten percent of the work — store submission, Arabic layout fixes, handover — actually happens.

Should support be a monthly retainer or paid per request?

For a live app with real users, a monthly tier is cheaper and faster, because fixes do not wait on a quote. A care tier of KWD 150–300 covers crashes, OS updates and store compliance. Pay-per-request only makes sense for an internal tool nobody depends on daily.
