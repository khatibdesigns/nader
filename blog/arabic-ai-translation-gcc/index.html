Arabic AI Translation: What It Gets Right and Wrong

13 October 2026 · 4 min read · Khatib Designs

Arabic AI translation has quietly become good enough for most of what a GCC business publishes — and still bad enough, in specific places, to embarrass a brand. The difference is not the model you pick. It is whether you decide in advance which content a machine can finish alone, which needs a human pass, and which should never be machine-translated at all.

Where Arabic AI translation already works, and where it breaks

For internal documents, support replies, product descriptions and first drafts, Arabic AI translation now gets you 80-90% of the way in seconds. That is a genuine shift from five years ago, when machine translation output for Arabic needed rewriting more often than editing. Grammar is rarely the problem anymore.

What still breaks is everything downstream of grammar: tone, terminology, and the conventions a Kuwaiti or Saudi reader expects. A model will happily produce correct Arabic that reads like a government circular when you wanted a friendly WhatsApp reply, or translate your product name into something meaningless. So the practical rule we use with clients: let AI carry volume and speed, and keep a human on anything that carries your logo, your price or your legal exposure.

The four failure modes we see most in GCC Arabic

  • Formal Arabic where dialect belongs. Ask for Arabic and you get Modern Standard Arabic. That is right for a policy page, a contract or a press release. It is wrong for customer chat, where Kuwaiti or Gulf phrasing makes the difference between helpful and stiff. Decide the register per channel, not per company.
  • Terms that should never be translated. Brand names, product SKUs, medicine names, plan tiers and feature labels get translated by default unless you lock them. This is the single most common cause of an Arabic page that looks careless.
  • Numbers, direction and layout. Currency placement for KWD and SAR, Arabic-Indic versus Latin digits, dates, phone numbers, and Latin strings inside right-to-left text that flip order on render. These are not translation bugs — they are formatting bugs that only appear in the Arabic build.
  • High-risk content. Dosage instructions, insurance exclusions, legal terms, HR policy. The cost of one wrong word is not a bad sentence, it is a claim.

Tier your content before you buy any tool

Most Arabic localisation projects stall because the team treats all content as one job. Split it into three tiers instead. Tier 1 is machine-only: internal notes, knowledge base drafts, long-tail product data, support macros that a human will see before the customer does. Tier 2 is machine plus a light human edit: marketing pages, app strings, email sequences, help articles. Tier 3 is written by a human in Arabic from the start: homepage copy, pricing pages, anything legal, anything medical.

Two assets make Tier 2 cheap. The first is a glossary of 50 to 200 locked terms — your brand language, product names and the Arabic you have already chosen for recurring UI words. The second is a translation memory, so the second thousand strings cost far less than the first. Platform translation APIs such as Google Cloud Translation support both glossaries and custom models, and the same pattern works with a prompt-based pipeline if your terminology is enforced rather than suggested.

When we built Dwa, a bilingual pharmacy delivery product in Kuwait, the hard part was never sentence translation. It was keeping medicine names, dosage text and status labels exactly consistent across both languages, in an interface that mirrors direction. That is a glossary-and-QA problem, not a model problem.

Wire it into the systems you already run

Translation delivers value when it stops being a project and becomes a step inside an existing flow: a new product gets its Arabic description when it is created, a support ticket gets an Arabic draft reply before an agent opens it, a newsletter ships in both languages from one brief. If you already send volume in English, AI email automation is usually the fastest place to prove the workflow, because the output is reviewable and the volume is real.

If the Arabic has to appear inside a product you already own, the integration question matters more than the translation question — which strings are extracted, where the glossary lives, how a fix propagates. How to check quality without a linguistics team

Automated scores are weak for marketing Arabic. A simple manual loop beats them. Pull 30 random translated strings per content type. Have one Arabic-native reviewer score each on two axes, one to five: accuracy against the source, and fluency as Arabic a customer would accept. Record how many strings needed any edit at all. That edit rate is your real number — if it sits under 10% for Tier 2, your glossary is working and you can scale volume. If it sits above 30%, the problem is almost always missing terminology or the wrong register, not the model.

Re-run the same sample after every change to your glossary or prompt. Thirty minutes a month of structured review will tell you more about your Arabic quality than any vendor benchmark.

A sensible first month

Pick one content type with real volume and low risk — support replies, product descriptions or help articles. Build the glossary from the 100 terms that actually recur. Translate a batch, measure the edit rate, fix the glossary, translate again. Only once that loop is stable should you touch the homepage, the pricing page or anything a lawyer reads.

Frequently asked questions

Is Arabic AI translation accurate enough to publish without review?

For low-risk content seen internally first, yes. For anything customer-facing with your brand, price or legal terms on it, no — budget a light human edit. The deciding factor is risk, not word count.

Should our Arabic be Modern Standard or Kuwaiti dialect?

Both, by channel. Use Modern Standard Arabic for pages, policies and contracts, and Gulf or Kuwaiti phrasing for chat, WhatsApp and support replies where formal Arabic reads cold.

What does a bilingual content workflow cost to set up?

The cost sits in the glossary, the integration and the review loop rather than per-word translation. A single content type with a locked glossary is usually a small scoped engagement; see our pricing guides for ranges.
